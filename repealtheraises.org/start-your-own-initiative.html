Who Can Use This

Any Ohio statutory city or village follows the process below — it comes straight from the Ohio Revised Code, not from a local charter. Most charter cities also follow this process unless their charter specifically replaced it with its own initiative/referendum procedure (check your city's charter or ask your law director/clerk). Home-rule townships have a similar option under R.C. 504.14.

Two Different Tools: Initiative vs. Referendum

Ohio law gives citizens two distinct petition tools, and picking the right one matters — we learned this the hard way (story below).

Initiative — Form 6-I Referendum — Form 6-J
What it does Lets citizens write and propose a brand-new ordinance — including one that repeals and replaces an existing one — and put it directly to voters. Lets citizens force an ordinance council already passed onto the ballot for voters to approve or reject as-is.
Signatures required 10% of the electors who voted for governor in the most recent gubernatorial election, in that city/village. 35% of the electors who voted for governor in the most recent gubernatorial election, in that city/village.
Deadline to file No fixed short fuse — but it must be certified at least 90 days before the general election you're targeting. Only 30 days after the ordinance is filed with the mayor (or passed over a veto). Miss it and the referendum option is gone.
Legal citation Ohio Revised Code §731.28 Ohio Revised Code §731.29
Official form Form 6-I — Initiative Petition (PDF) Form 6-J — Referendum Petition (search "Form 6-J" on the Ohio Secretary of State's Forms & Petitions page)

What happens after a petition is certified — effective dates, how the board of elections handles the vote, and the process for both tools — is covered in Ohio Revised Code §731.31.

Our Story: Why We Failed in 2023 and Won in 2026

When Warren's City Council passed the pay raise ordinance in the final days of 2023, our first move was a referendum petition — the tool that lets voters reject an ordinance council already passed. The problem: a referendum only gives you 30 days from the ordinance's passage, and you need signatures from 35% of the electors who voted for governor. Our 30-day clock ran straight through the holidays — filing logistics, weekends, and Christmas ate most of our usable days, leaving us with far less than 30 actual working days to gather signatures at that threshold. We came up short.

So we switched tools. Instead of asking voters to reject the existing ordinance, we filed an initiative petition — a new ordinance we wrote ourselves that repeals and replaces the raise. The initiative route only requires 10% of the gubernatorial vote and isn't tied to a 30-day fuse after passage, just a 90-day runway before the election you're targeting. That's the version that got certified for the November 3, 2026 ballot. See the press release for the full timeline.

Writing Your Own Petition: Title & Ordinance Text

Both the state's official form and Ohio law require more than just signatures. Before you circulate:

  • File a certified copy first. For an initiative petition, a certified copy of your proposed ordinance must be filed with the City Auditor, Village Clerk, or Township Fiscal Officer before you circulate it for signatures.
  • Write a real ordinance, not just a slogan. Form 6-I requires "a full and correct copy of the title and text of the proposed Ordinance" printed on every part-petition. Keep the title short and plain (ours was framed as rescinding and replacing the existing pay-raise ordinance) — voters and circulators need to understand at a glance what it does. Have an attorney or your law director review the operative text; sloppy drafting is one of the easiest ways for a petition to be challenged.
  • Name a committee. The petition must designate a committee of petitioners responsible for filing it and standing in for the signers if it's challenged.
  • Keep signatures clean. Signatures on any one part-petition must come from a single county, be written in ink, and be from actual registered electors of your city or village — invalid signatures (wrong address, not registered, duplicates) get thrown out during board of elections review, so gather more than your bare minimum.

Full instructions and statutory citations are printed directly on Form 6-I itself.

Find Your Numbers

Both thresholds (10% for initiative, 35% for referendum) are based on the number of electors who voted for governor in your city or village at the most recent gubernatorial election — not registered voters, not turnout in a random election. That baseline resets after every governor's race. Ohio's next gubernatorial election is in 2026; a well-contested race typically produces a bigger, harder-to-hit denominator than a quiet one, so the exact signature count you'll need can change significantly from cycle to cycle.

Your county board of elections can tell you the exact number for your municipality. Find yours at the Ohio Secretary of State's County Boards of Elections directory.

Learn the Legal Standards

The Ohio Secretary of State publishes a comprehensive Ohio Ballot Questions and Issues Handbook covering the legal rulings and standards election officials apply to petitions like these — read it before you draft anything.

When you have specific questions, talk to your local board of elections first, or call the Ohio Secretary of State's Elections Administration division directly at 614-466-2655.

Don't Live in Ohio?

Most states give citizens some version of this power — it just goes by different names and different rules. Search "[your state] municipal initiative petition" or "[your city] citizens' referendum process" to find your state's equivalent statute, or call your county/city clerk and ask directly how residents can petition to put a local ordinance on the ballot.
